# Internal audit of the model, the code and the paper

Date: 11 October 2026. Scope: `code/` in full (16 files), `paper.md` in full,
every table in `results/` cross-checked against the numbers quoted in the text.

Nothing here says a conclusion is wrong. Most of it says a conclusion is stated
more confidently than the evidence behind it supports, or rests on a step that
is in the code but not in the paper. They are ordered by how much they would
change a careful reader's reading.

---

## A. Substantive

### A1. The exposure-response curve is an interpolation of two points, then extrapolated several-fold beyond them

`05_delivery.py` fits an Emax curve with two free parameters, EC₅₀ and a Hill
coefficient, to exactly two published mouse doses. Two parameters on two points
is an exact interpolation: `results/emax_fit.csv` shows the fitted values equal
the measured values to the decimal, because there is no residual to minimise and
zero degrees of freedom. The Hill coefficient of 1.215 is not an estimate of
cooperativity; it is whatever value makes the curve pass through both points.

The two points span 7.5 to 25 nmol/g. The curve is then used at 4.1 nmol/g
(putamen bulk), at 61.8 nmol/g (the 90% knockdown target), and in the dosing
analysis at steady-state peaks up to 157 nmol/g, which is six times the highest
calibration point.

Everything in the delivery and dosing half of the paper passes through this
curve: the whole of Table V, the 25 nmol/g requirement, the 7 mg against 7.6 g
contrast, and every row of the dosing-schedule analysis.

Methods 2.9 says the curve was "fitted to the two published mouse
intracerebroventricular doses", which is honest about the count. It does not say
that two parameters on two points cannot be wrong on their own data, and it does
not say the curve is extrapolated.

**Suggested fix.** State the zero degrees of freedom explicitly, mark the
calibrated range on Figure 9A, and either restrict the quantitative claims to
that range or carry a sensitivity band on the Hill coefficient.

### A2. The primate engagement figure is a different measurement than the one it is used as

`hdmodel.py` records the primate numbers as "residual target RNA in putamen
SPNs". The paper presents them in a table whose column reads "Knockdown
achieved", in a section about MSH3, and Methods 2.9 says "measured primate
target engagement was used directly".

Two substitutions happen silently between the source and the claim. The paper
never states which transcript the primate study measured, or whether it was
MSH3. And residual *RNA* is used where the Emax curve expects residual
*protein*, which are different quantities with different kinetics.

If the primate study measured a different target, then "29% MSH3 knockdown in
putaminal projection neurons" is really "an oligonucleotide of comparable
chemistry achieved 29% knockdown of its own target in that cell type, and MSH3
is assumed to behave the same way". That is a reasonable assumption and a
standard one, but it is the load-bearing step of the entire delivery argument
and it is invisible in the paper.

**Suggested fix.** Name the transcript, state the RNA-to-protein substitution,
and move both into Limitations.

### A3. The first-order MSH3 result is close to circular as currently tested

`01_calibrate.py` scores k = 1, 1.5, 2 and 3 by RMSE against
`prevention_from_msh3` evaluated on `np.linspace(0, 100, 21)`. That comparator
is the published linear regression, not the data. So:

- the test compares candidate curves to a straight line;
- of the orders tested, only k = 1 is itself a straight line in m;
- 19 of the 21 comparison points are regression values at MSH3 levels where no
  measurement exists, and all 21 are weighted equally;
- the only two real points sit at 25% and 59%.

A test that asks "which of these curves is most like a line" and offers exactly
one line will return that line. The reported spread, RMSE 4.97 for k = 1 against
26.43 for k = 3, therefore overstates the evidence considerably.

The conclusion is probably still right. The two raw points do discriminate:
measured 78.1% prevention at 25% residual MSH3, against 75.0% for k = 1 and
98.4% for k = 3. That is a real argument and it is in the paper. It is just much
weaker than the RMSE table implies, and it rests on two points from a regression
with R² = 0.65.

**Suggested fix.** Lead with the two measured points, report the RMSE table as
a secondary check with its circularity stated, and say plainly that the evidence
excludes a strongly cooperative species rather than establishing exact
first-order behaviour.

### A4. The sharpness of the switch is assumed, and the headline claim depends on it

The paper's central structural claim is that the length dependence is a switch
rather than a ramp. The switch has two properties: where it sits, and how sharp
it is.

Its position, `L_sw`, is fitted and is tightly determined: 78.3 to 81.7 repeats
at a 10% tolerance. That part of the claim is earned.

Its sharpness, `w_sw`, is not fitted. `_params_from_vector` reads it from
`ASSUMED["w_switch"] = 6.0` before the optimiser ever runs, and `10_profile.py`
profiles only λ(40), β, R and L_sw. So how switch-like the switch is was decided
in advance, not inferred. The sensitivity analysis sweeps it over 3 to 12
repeats and finds PRCC −0.42, making it the sixth strongest driver in the model.

**Suggested fix.** Either fit `w_sw` as a fifth parameter, which the four
anchors will not support, or profile it the way the others are profiled and
report the range over which the fit survives. At minimum, say in Results 3.1
that the sharpness is an input.

### A5. Three assumed parameters are among the strongest drivers, and the text reads wider than it is

Results 3.13 says: "The elimination submodel constants, which are the parameters
that were simply assumed with no data behind them, all have partial rank
correlations below 0.18. The conclusions do not rest on them."

That sentence is true of the three elimination constants. But they are not the
only assumed parameters, and the sentence's apposition invites the reader to
think they are. From `results/sensitivity_prcc.csv`, ranked by effect on
benefit:

| input | PRCC | kind |
|---|---|---|
| msh3_order | +0.73 | anchor |
| step_repeats | −0.71 | anchor |
| **contraction_ratio** | **−0.68** | **assumed** |
| t_40_60_years | −0.62 | anchor |
| **contraction_step** | **−0.45** | **assumed** |
| **w_switch** | **−0.42** | **assumed** |

The paragraph above names the contraction rate as a top driver without
mentioning that it is assumed. Figure 15B colours it red for "assumed", so the
figure is honest and the prose is not.

**Suggested fix.** Say that three assumed parameters sit in the top six, name
them, and restrict the reassurance to the elimination submodel where it holds.

### A6. "Every assumed value is varied in the global sensitivity analysis" is not true

Methods 2.1 states it, and Table III repeats it as "assumed, all swept". The
global analysis in `07_sensitivity.py` sweeps fifteen inputs. Not among them:

- `ranen_mean_parent_aao` (45 years), which see A7;
- `L_max` (1000 repeats), named in Results 3.3 as the cause of the one
  validation row that misses, and never varied to demonstrate it;
- every delivery and translation constant: brain and striatum mass, body
  weight, putaminal depth, plasma volume.

Molecular weight and endosomal escape are swept, but one at a time inside
dedicated figures, not in the global analysis. The claim should be narrowed to
what is actually true: the global analysis covers the expansion and elimination
model, and the delivery constants are varied separately and one at a time.

### A7. A headline falsifiable prediction rests on an assumed number that appears nowhere in the paper

Two significant results use the Orth and Schwenke onset regression: the
rejection of the faster model M2 in Results 3.5, and the falsifiable prediction
that clinical onset corresponds to 18 to 31% neuron loss.

That regression needs a parental age at onset. The code supplies 45 years, with
the comment "ASSUMED cohort mean, used only for a sanity check". The paper uses
it for considerably more than a sanity check, and the number appears neither in
the text nor in Table III nor in the sensitivity sweep.

The sensitivity is moderate: the coefficient is 0.3293, so ±5 years of parental
onset moves predicted onset by ±1.6 years, and 46.4 years becomes 44.8 or 48.1.
That is not enough to rescue M2, which fails by 40 percentage points of neuron
loss, so the conclusion holds. But an undisclosed assumption behind a stated
falsifiable prediction is exactly what a reviewer looks for.

**Suggested fix.** State the assumption where the regression is first used, add
it to Table III, and add it to the sweep.

## B. Missing explanations

### B1. The mouse and primate arms treat the same physics differently

For the primate route the analysis refuses to assume uniform distribution and
instead infers a 3.9 mm penetration length from the reported regional gradient,
which is the right instinct. For the mouse route the ICV dose is converted to
tissue exposure as dose divided by whole-brain mass, which assumes an
intraventricular injection distributes uniformly through the brain. It does not,
and the same gradient logic would apply. The inconsistency is never addressed.

### B2. The solver check is noise-limited and does not converge

From `results/solver_agreement.csv`, the largest survival difference at 42
repeats runs 0.034, 0.020, 0.023 as the step halves from 1/12 to 1/48 of a year.
It stops falling. That is Monte Carlo scatter between two stochastic runs, not
discretisation error, which means the comparison cannot detect a tau-leaping
bias smaller than about two to three percentage points. Methods 2.5 quotes the
numbers accurately but describes the result as verification without saying what
the test can and cannot resolve.

### B3. The shape of the step-size distribution is unexplained

Methods justify the mean of four repeats from the reconstituted system. Nothing
justifies choosing a geometric distribution rather than any other with that
mean. The choice sets the tail, and the tail is precisely where the one failing
validation row sits: terminal lengths reach 375 in the model against observed
values beyond 500.

### B4. Population size varies between scripts without explanation

9,000 in the dosing analysis, 12,000 in the modifier and power analyses, 15,000
in the subtype analysis, 20,000 in the main validation. Each is presumably a
runtime compromise, but since Monte Carlo noise is about a year on the age
endpoints and scales with the count, the reader should be told why each number
was chosen.

### B5. Calibration fits a mean-field integral to stochastic means

Transit times are computed as T = ∫dL/(s(L)·λ(L)). The published anchors are
mean first-passage times of a jump process. For a nonlinear λ these are not the
same quantity. Methods say the integral was "checked afterwards against the
stochastic simulation", and a solver comparison does exist, but it compares
survival curves and ages at 50% loss, not transit times. A direct comparison of
the four calibration targets between the two methods is the check the sentence
promises and I could not find it.

### B6. The subtype rate difference is implemented as a rate-constant multiplier

`11_subtypes.py` implements "15% faster expansion" as A × 1.15, a uniform
multiplier at every tract length. The reported 15% is a difference in a measured
somatic expansion index, which is not the same object as the rate constant.
Reasonable, standard, and unstated.

## C. Minor

- **C1.** `hdmodel.py:208` refers to `02_spn_simulation.py`, which does not
  exist. The solver check lives in `03_coupling.py`.
- **C2.** Runtime is "about 30 minutes" in Methods 2.1 and "about forty minutes"
  in Section 6 and the README.
- **C3.** `ASSUMED["L_switch"] = 80.0` and `ASSUMED["R_cluster"] = 10.0` are
  commented as placeholders but are live fallbacks: any call to `lambda_L` or
  `clustered_fraction` that omits `L_sw` or `R` silently gets them instead of
  the fitted 80.2 and 17.7. Every current caller passes them, so nothing is
  wrong today, but it is a quiet trap. They should raise instead.
- **C4.** Results 3.3 says four of five held-out observations are reproduced.
  Row one is 99.9% against a published 95 to 98%, which is outside the range.
  The table shows it plainly; the prose rounds it up to a pass.
- **C5.** The `tot <= 0` branch in `simulate_cell` is unreachable, since λ is
  strictly positive everywhere.
- **C6.** `L_max = 1000` is named as the reason the terminal-length tail is too
  narrow but is never varied to show that it is the reason.

## What was checked and found sound

- Every number quoted in Results 3.10, 3.11, 3.12 and 3.7 matches the
  corresponding result table exactly.
- The negative-binomial step accumulation in the tau-leaping solver is correct:
  a sum of n geometric draws is n plus a negative binomial, and the code
  implements exactly that.
- The reparameterisation in `phi_slipout` is algebraically consistent with the
  two-state derivation in its docstring.
- The censoring logic added to the trial endpoints is correct, and censored
  values are reported as bounds everywhere they appear.
- Methods 2.4 is careful and correct about what the profile ranges are and are
  not, and explicitly declines to call them confidence intervals.
- The reconciliation in Results 3.5 tests three candidate explanations, reports
  that all three fail or create a worse problem, and does not pick a winner. It
  is the most honest section in the paper.
